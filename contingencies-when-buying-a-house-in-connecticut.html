Contingencies When Buying a House in Connecticut

In Connecticut, the contingencies in your purchase contract are your financial safety net. Here's when to keep them — and when waiving one might actually make sense.

Published · August 1, 2026 at 10:12 AM ET

In most Connecticut real estate contracts, buyers write in contingencies that let them exit — with their deposit intact — if a home inspection uncovers major problems, their lender doesn't come through, or the property appraises below the purchase price. In Stamford's seller's market, where Redfin reported a median sale price near $712,000 as of May 2026 and roughly six in ten homes sold above asking, those protections are sometimes the first thing buyers offer to drop. This page explains what each contingency does, what you lose when you waive it, and how to decide what's actually worth the risk.

What a Contingency Actually Does

A contingency is a condition written into your purchase contract. If that condition isn't met, you can walk away and get your earnest money back — typically without penalty. Remove the condition, and your deposit is at risk if things go sideways.

That's it. Nothing exotic. But the real-world dollar consequences of keeping or dropping one can run well into five figures, which is why this decision deserves more thought than most buyers give it.

The Inspection Contingency

This is the one most buyers think of first. It gives you the right to have a licensed home inspector evaluate the property — and depending on what's found, to request repairs, a price reduction, a credit at closing, or to cancel the deal entirely.

In Connecticut, the inspection window is typically 7 to 14 days from the accepted offer date, with sellers customarily responding within 3 to 7 days after that.

A few things worth knowing:

  • Older Stamford housing stock is not forgiving. Many homes in Stamford and surrounding Fairfield County towns were built before 1960. Oil tanks, knob-and-tube wiring, asbestos, and aging sewer laterals turn up more often than buyers expect. Skipping an inspection on a 70-year-old colonial is a very different risk than skipping one on a 2019 condo.
  • Informational-only is a real middle ground. Rather than waiving the inspection outright, many buyers in competitive situations waive the right to request repairs — but still complete the inspection for their own knowledge. You keep eyes on the property; the seller gets certainty. This approach became increasingly common in Stamford through 2025 and into 2026.
  • A capped repair ask is another option. Some buyers agree upfront to only request repairs if costs exceed a set threshold. That limits your exit to genuinely catastrophic finds while giving the seller confidence you won't nickel-and-dime over worn caulk or a cracked downspout.

For more on what inspectors focus on locally, see our Stamford buyer guides.

The Mortgage (Financing) Contingency

A financing contingency says the deal is only binding if you secure a loan commitment by a specified date — usually 30 to 45 days from the accepted offer in Connecticut, though this window is often compressed in competitive offers.

This is the one contingency most buyers should not waive unless they are paying all cash or have exceptional certainty about their loan situation.

Here's why: a pre-approval letter is not a loan commitment. Underwriting can unearth problems — a change in employment, a new credit inquiry, undisclosed debt, or a property condition flagged by the lender's own review. Waiving the financing contingency means you could lose your earnest money even if the deal collapses through no real fault of your own.

If you want to strengthen your offer without waiving this contingency, the better move is to arrive with a thorough pre-approval from a lender who has already pulled your income documentation, tax returns, and full asset picture — not just run a soft credit check. The Appraisal Contingency

When you're financing a home, your lender orders an independent appraisal. If the appraised value comes in below the purchase price, the lender will base the loan on the lower number — leaving a gap you'd have to cover in cash, renegotiate with the seller, or walk away from.

An appraisal contingency protects you in that scenario: if the home doesn't appraise at the contract price, you can renegotiate or exit with your deposit intact.

In Stamford's market, appraisal gaps are a real risk. When homes regularly sell 5–10% above asking price, appraisals — which look backward at comparable closed sales — can lag behind what buyers are actually paying today. If you waive this contingency and a gap appears, you need cash reserves to cover it. That's a legitimate strategy for a well-capitalized buyer; it's a dangerous one for someone already stretched on the down payment.

A practical middle ground: an appraisal gap clause, where you agree to cover any shortfall up to a specified dollar amount without renegotiation. This signals seriousness to the seller while capping your exposure at a number you've already decided you can absorb.

How Stamford's Market Shapes These Decisions

As of mid-2026, Stamford remains a competitive market. Redfin data through May 2026 shows a median sale price near $712,000, with roughly six in ten homes selling above asking and a sale-to-list ratio around 104%. Inventory has been tight — down roughly 15% through 2025 compared to the prior year, per William Pitt Sotheby's market data. Average homes sell in around 30 days; the most competitive listings go pending in under three weeks.

Multiple-offer situations are the norm on well-priced homes. That pressure tempts buyers to strip out contingencies to make their offers look cleaner to sellers.

Here's the counterintuitive truth: the stronger your financial position, the more comfortable you can afford to be when modifying contingencies — because you have reserves to absorb a bad outcome. The weaker your financial position, the more you need those contingencies, because you don't have a cushion. Yet it's often the buyers with the least cushion who feel the most pressure to waive. Don't let a bidding war push you into a risk profile that doesn't match your actual finances. For a current read on what's driving competition in this market, check the Stamford Market Overview.

A Practical Framework Before You Decide

Before modifying any contingency, answer these questions honestly:

  • Inspection: How old is the property, and what systems are you most worried about? Could you absorb a surprise repair bill of $20,000 or more? If yes, an informational-only inspection is reasonable. If no, keep the full contingency.
  • Financing: Has your lender fully underwritten your file, or just issued a soft pre-approval? Do you have a fallback if the loan falls through? If you can't answer both confidently, keep this contingency in place.
  • Appraisal: What's the most you could cover out of pocket if the home appraises $25,000–$50,000 below the contract price? An appraisal gap clause up to that amount is a reasonable concession. Beyond it, keep the full contingency.

The goal isn't to write the most aggressive offer in the pile. The goal is to write an offer you can actually close — and not regret if the inspector finds a failed septic system or the lender's appraiser sees the comp set differently than your agent did.

FAQ

Can I waive the inspection contingency in Connecticut?

Yes — it's legal and it happens in competitive markets like Stamford. But waiving it entirely means you have no right to cancel or renegotiate based on what the inspector finds. A stronger middle path, especially on older homes, is completing the inspection for informational purposes only: you keep eyes on the property without holding the seller to a repair negotiation.

What happens if the appraisal comes in below the purchase price in Connecticut?

If your contract includes an appraisal contingency, you can renegotiate the price, cover the gap in cash, or exit and recover your deposit. If you've waived it, you're generally committed to closing regardless — meaning you bring extra cash to the table or forfeit your earnest money. In Stamford's market, where homes often sell 5–10% above asking, this gap risk is real and worth planning for before you make an offer.

How long does a financing contingency typically last in Connecticut?

Financing contingencies in Connecticut typically run 30 to 45 days from the accepted offer date. In competitive situations, buyers sometimes offer a shorter window — around 21 days — to sweeten the deal. That only works if your lender can genuinely move that fast with a fully underwritten file. Agreeing to a timeline your lender can't meet just trades one problem for another.
