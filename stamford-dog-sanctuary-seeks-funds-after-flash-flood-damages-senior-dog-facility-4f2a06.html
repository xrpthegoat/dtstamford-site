Stamford Dog Sanctuary Seeks Funds After Flash Flood Damages Senior Dog Facility

Stamford nonprofit dog sanctuary fights to recover after flash flood destroys its operational space, relying on community support to rebuild.

Uploaded · August 5, 2026 at 7:39 AM ET

A local nonprofit dog sanctuary in Stamford has launched a fundraising campaign after a flash flood on July 21, 2026, severely damaged its operating space. The Special Needs Senior Dog Sanctuary, based in a residential home, cares for 13 medically fragile senior dogs and relies on a fully functional lower level for daily operations. The flood destroyed flooring, drywall, insulation, doors, baseboards, laundry equipment, and dog living areas—critical infrastructure for the sanctuary’s mission. With flood insurance denying coverage due to the space being classified as a “basement” despite being fully finished and used daily, the nonprofit is turning to the community for support. As of July 29, 2026, a GoFundMe campaign had raised nearly $23,000 of a $26,000 goal, with 221 donors contributing [1][3].

What's happening

On July 21, 2026, a flash flood caused by severe storms and nearly 5 inches of rain inundated the lower level of the home where the Special Needs Senior Dog Sanctuary operates [2]. The nonprofit, founded by Todd and Anna Giorgi in 2012, has rescued 44 senior dogs with special needs over 14 years and currently provides lifelong care for 13 dogs [1][3]. The flood damaged or destroyed the entire lower-level infrastructure, including walls, drywall, insulation, flooring, doors, baseboards, dog living areas, storage, and essential equipment such as a dryer used daily to wash bedding and blankets [3]. The sanctuary’s GoFundMe campaign, launched by Todd Giorgi, aims to raise $15,000 to cover water damage remediation, drywall and insulation replacement, flooring, electrical repairs, and replacement of laundry equipment and damaged sanctuary supplies and equipment [3]. The nonprofit emphasized that the space is not a basement in function or use—Stamford taxes the property as a four-bedroom home—but flood insurance policies classify it as such, excluding coverage [2][3]. The group stated, “For 14 years, we’ve said ‘yes’ to the dogs no one else wanted. Today, we’re asking for help so we can continue saying ‘yes’” [3].

Community response and ongoing recovery

The community has responded with strong support. As of July 29, 2026, the GoFundMe campaign had raised nearly $14,700 from 151 donors, according to the Stamford Advocate [3]. The sanctuary has also shared updates via social media, urging supporters to share the fundraiser and emphasizing that the damage was not just to property but to the home of 13 dogs who depend on daily care [4]. The nonprofit confirmed that the space is currently unusable and that restoration is urgent to ensure the continued safety and well-being of the dogs. No official timeline for repairs has been released, but the organization is actively seeking funds to begin remediation.

What this means for Stamford buyers & renters

This incident highlights a growing vulnerability in Stamford’s housing stock—particularly in older, mixed-use residential properties—when it comes to flood risk and insurance limitations. While the sanctuary’s situation is extreme, it underscores a broader reality: many homes in Stamford, especially those with finished basements or lower levels used for non-residential purposes, may face similar challenges during heavy rainfall events. As climate patterns shift and storms become more intense, even moderate flooding can cause disproportionate damage to spaces that are legally classified as basements, regardless of their actual use or value to the household.

For renters, this serves as a reminder that the condition of a unit’s lower level—especially in older buildings—can be a key factor in long-term livability. If a unit is located on a lower floor or in a basement, it may be more susceptible to water intrusion during storms, even if it’s well-maintained. Tenants should consider this when evaluating rental properties, particularly in areas with known drainage issues or proximity to stormwater runoff zones.

For buyers, the story reinforces the importance of understanding a property’s flood risk and insurance classification. A home with a finished lower level used for business, storage, or even a sanctuary may not be covered by standard flood insurance, even if it’s fully habitable and taxed as a full residential unit. This can create financial exposure for owners who invest in such spaces. Buyers should request flood zone maps, review insurance policy terms, and consider the long-term maintenance and resilience of a property’s infrastructure—especially in neighborhoods with aging stormwater systems.

Moreover, the outpouring of community support reflects a strong local culture of care and resilience in Stamford. The fact that a nonprofit dedicated to senior dogs can mobilize nearly $15,000 in just over a week speaks to the city’s capacity for collective action. This kind of civic engagement can influence neighborhood dynamics, encouraging more community-driven solutions to infrastructure and environmental challenges. As Stamford continues to grow and adapt, properties that support community-serving functions—like this sanctuary—may gain added value, not just for their residents but for the broader city.

In a city where walkability, transit access, and neighborhood character are key selling points, the ability to live in a place where people care deeply about shared spaces and vulnerable populations is increasingly meaningful. This story reminds us that even in a high-demand real estate market, the human and animal elements of a community are central to what makes Stamford a place people want to live.

Common questions

Why doesn’t the sanctuary’s flood insurance cover the damage?

The space is legally classified as a basement despite being fully finished and used daily, which excludes it from standard flood insurance coverage, even though it’s taxed as a four-bedroom home.

How does this affect Stamford renters and buyers?

Renters should assess lower-level units for flood risk, especially in older buildings. Buyers must verify flood zone status and insurance coverage for finished basements used for non-residential purposes.

Can a finished basement in Stamford be covered by flood insurance?

In this case, the insurer classified the finished lower level as a basement and denied coverage. Coverage terms vary by policy and insurer — property owners should review their own policy with their insurer.
