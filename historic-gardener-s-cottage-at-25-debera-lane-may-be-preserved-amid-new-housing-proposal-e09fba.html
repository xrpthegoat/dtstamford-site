Historic Gardener’s Cottage at 25 DeBera Lane May Be Preserved Amid New Housing Proposal

A 1905 gardener’s cottage in Stamford’s East Side may be preserved amid a new housing project, reflecting a growing trend of integrating history into modern development.

Uploaded · July 30, 2026 at 12:52 PM ET

A small but historically significant structure at 25 DeBera Lane on Stamford’s East Side could be saved and integrated into a new residential development, offering a rare chance to preserve a piece of the city’s past while expanding housing options. For buyers and renters in Stamford, this proposal reflects a growing trend of balancing development with heritage — a dynamic that shapes neighborhood character and long-term value.

What's happening

A proposal submitted by developer Vanderson DiGesu seeks to preserve and renovate the facade of a house built in 1905 at 25 DeBera Lane, originally serving as a gardener’s cottage on the estate of Eva Weber [1]. The property, located near Courtland Avenue and East Main Street, is the last remaining structure from an 18-acre estate, having served as its gardener's cottage between 1905 and 1942 [1]. Weber donated the land to the city in the 1940s, which became Courtland Park [1]. The cottage is historically significant as the only surviving remnant of that estate and is considered representative of auxiliary housing common on large estates at the turn of the 20th century [1].

Under a city incentive program that encourages the preservation of historic structures, the developer plans to retain and restore over 50% of the original building [1]. Work would include replacing vinyl siding, restoring the decorative entryway, and recreating missing period details [1]. Behind the preserved facade, two new residential units would be constructed, increasing housing density on the site beyond what standard zoning would allow [1]. The project is being reviewed under a program that allows developers to build at higher density in exchange for protecting historically important buildings [1].

The proposal is part of a broader effort to maintain the visual and cultural continuity of Stamford’s older neighborhoods while addressing demand for housing. The developer’s application emphasizes that the new community will preserve the existing streetscape and provide homes for three families in a quiet, green area [1]. The city Planning Board is expected to review the proposal at its Aug. 11 meeting; a construction timeline has not been disclosed.

The broader context of preservation and development in Stamford

Stamford has long balanced rapid growth with efforts to retain its architectural and cultural identity. As the city continues to attract professionals and families seeking urban amenities with a suburban feel, the pressure to build new housing increases. Yet, many residents and local historians value the city’s historic character — especially in neighborhoods like the East Side, where older homes and estate remnants still exist.

This proposal at 25 DeBera Lane exemplifies a shift in how development is being approached: not as replacement, but as integration. Rather than demolishing older structures, developers are increasingly finding ways to incorporate them into new projects. This model supports both the city’s preservation goals and its housing needs. It also reflects a growing awareness among buyers and renters that neighborhoods with historical continuity often have stronger long-term appeal and stability.

For renters, projects like this signal a future where older parts of Stamford aren’t being erased by new construction, but instead enhanced. The presence of preserved historic elements can contribute to a sense of place and neighborhood identity — qualities that are increasingly sought after in a city where new buildings often look similar. For buyers, especially those interested in homes with character, such developments may offer a rare opportunity to live in a space that blends history with modern convenience.

The fact that the city is offering density incentives for preservation suggests that this approach is not just a niche effort but a strategic part of Stamford’s planning framework. It indicates that the city values architectural continuity and is willing to reward developers who prioritize it. This kind of policy can influence how future projects are designed, potentially leading to more thoughtful integration of old and new across the city.

While the exact number of units, pricing, or final design details have not been released, the fact that a century-old cottage is being preserved amid new construction is a meaningful signal. It shows that even in a high-demand market, there is room for heritage — and that preservation can coexist with growth.

What this means for Stamford buyers & renters

In today’s Stamford market, where demand for housing remains strong and rental prices are elevated, developments like this one are more than just construction projects — they’re cultural markers. They reflect a city that’s trying to grow without losing its soul. For renters, this kind of project means that even in a tight market, there may be opportunities to live in neighborhoods where history is respected and the built environment feels intentional, not generic.

For buyers, the presence of preserved historic elements in new developments can signal long-term neighborhood stability. Many buyers are drawn to neighborhoods that blend old and new, as they often feel more unique and connected to the past. The fact that a 1905 cottage is being saved suggests that Stamford is still investing in its identity — a quality that can make a neighborhood more resilient to market fluctuations.

Moreover, this proposal underscores a trend: the value of walkability, green space, and neighborhood character is rising. Even in a city with strong transit access and downtown proximity, people are increasingly drawn to areas that feel lived-in, layered, and connected to the past. The East Side, with its mix of older homes, tree-lined streets, and proximity to parks, is a prime example of a neighborhood where such qualities are still intact.

As Stamford continues to grow, projects like this one at 25 DeBera Lane offer a model for how development can be both responsible and forward-looking. They remind us that preserving history isn’t about slowing progress — it’s about building a future that remembers where we came from.
